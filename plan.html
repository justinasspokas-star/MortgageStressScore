Part 1 — Understand your situation

  1. Find your servicer. Use your latest mortgage statement and find the official customer-service number, not a phone number from an advertisement.
  2. Confirm your loan status. Record whether you are current, how many installments are missed, and what the servicer says is past due.
  3. Identify deadlines. Save every notice and date, especially any court response, loss-mitigation or foreclosure sale deadlines.
  4. Make a realistic budget. Write monthly income and essential expenses, including taxes, insurance and HOA dues. Avoid guessing.
  5. Calculate the shortfall. Compare your regular mortgage payment with what you can actually pay now; separate a temporary gap from a persistent affordability problem.

Part 2 — Prepare your documents

  1. Write your hardship timeline. Note when a job loss, income reduction, illness or increased expense started and whether you expect it to end.
  2. Gather income evidence. Save recent pay stubs, benefit statements, tax returns or self-employment records that the servicer requests.
  3. Gather mortgage records. Keep your most recent statement, notices, escrow statement and payment confirmations together.
  4. Record your monthly expenses. Include food, utilities, transport, childcare, medical expenses and other essential bills.
  5. Create a document log. Track file names, submission dates, confirmation numbers and any missing items identified by the servicer.

Part 3 — Ask for help

  1. Contact your mortgage servicer. Ask for available loss-mitigation options and how to start a complete application.
  2. Request a written checklist. Ask which documents, signatures and deadlines apply to your particular mortgage.
  3. Check free housing counseling. A HUD-approved foreclosure-prevention counselor can help explain options and paperwork, at no charge.
  4. Ask about your loan type. FHA, VA, USDA and conventional loans may follow different rules and investor requirements.
  5. Get the response in writing. Record what the servicer says about eligibility, trial payments, appeal or escalation processes.

Part 4 — Compare and follow up

  1. Compare complete payment terms. Include escrow, interest rate, repayment term, fees and any deferred or partial-claim balance.
  2. Ask about the end of forbearance. A payment pause usually does not erase the missed balance. Get the exit terms in writing.
  3. Review affordability over time. A smaller monthly payment can mean a longer loan or a higher total amount paid.
  4. Protect yourself from scams. Do not sign over your deed or pay a company promising guaranteed foreclosure rescue.
  5. Schedule follow-up. Record who you spoke to, when the next response is due, and whom to contact if nothing arrives. Seek legal help quickly if court deadlines apply.

Scripts and templates you can use

Servicer call script

“Hello, I’m contacting you about my mortgage because [brief reason]. I want to understand all loss-mitigation options available for my loan type. Please tell me what documents are required, the deadlines, whether my application is complete, and how I will receive a written decision. Can I have a reference number for this call?”

Hardship letter outline

Date: [date]\nTo: [servicer name]\nAccount reference: [last four digits only]\n\nI am requesting a review of available mortgage assistance options because [factual hardship] began on [date]. My current income situation is [short explanation], and I expect [temporary / ongoing / unknown] difficulty making payments.\n\nPlease provide the loss-mitigation options for my loan and a list of the documents required. I can be reached through the secure communication method already on file.\n\nSincerely,\n[name]

Questions before accepting any option

  • What will my complete payment be, including escrow?
  • What will I owe at sale, refinance or payoff?
  • Does it change my loan term or interest rate?
  • Is there a trial plan? What happens if I cannot complete it?
  • How does this affect credit reporting, and where are the written terms?
