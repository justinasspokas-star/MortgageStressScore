Behind on mortgage payments?

Answer first: If you are behind on mortgage payments, contact your mortgage servicer immediately, ask about loss-mitigation options, write down all deadlines, and consider speaking with a HUD-approved housing counselor. Do not ignore letters, do not pay upfront-fee rescue companies, and do not assume you must solve the full missed amount all at once. Options may include repayment plan, forbearance, deferral, loan modification, or other servicer programs depending on your loan.

Falling behind on a mortgage payment is stressful, but it does not mean there is only one path. The important thing is to act quickly, understand your timeline, and compare options before fees, missed notices, or legal deadlines make the situation harder to manage.

Last reviewed: May 2026 · About this site

Important: This guide is educational only. It is not legal, financial, tax, credit, mortgage, or foreclosure-prevention advice. If you have received legal notices or foreclosure documents, consider contacting your servicer, a HUD-approved housing counselor, and a qualified attorney in your state.

1. Confirm how far behind you are

Start by confirming the exact status of your loan. Are you late by a few days, one full payment, two or more payments, or already in a formal loss-mitigation or foreclosure process? The answer matters because servicer options and timelines can change as delinquency increases.

Check your servicer portal, recent statements, letters, fees, and payment history. Write down the total past-due amount, late fees, escrow shortages, and the next deadline. Do not rely only on memory or phone conversations.

2. Contact your mortgage servicer

Your servicer is the company that collects the payment and manages your account. Call or log in to the official servicer portal and say clearly: “I am behind on my mortgage payments and I want to understand what options are available for my loan.”

Ask for loss-mitigation, hardship assistance, or home retention options. Ask what documents are required, what deadlines apply, and whether any review can pause or affect collection activity. Keep a record of the date, representative name, and reference number for every conversation.

The options available depend on your loan type, servicer, investor rules, hardship reason, payment history, and income. Still, it helps to know the common categories before the call.

Repayment plan

You catch up missed payments over time by paying extra each month. It may fit if the hardship was temporary and income has recovered.

Forbearance

Your payment may be paused or reduced temporarily. It can help short-term, but missed amounts usually must be handled later.

Deferral

Some past-due amounts may be moved to a later date, depending on your loan rules and approval.

Loan modification

Your existing loan terms may be reviewed and adjusted if you qualify under hardship rules.

4. Understand whether the hardship is temporary or ongoing

A temporary hardship might be a short job gap, temporary medical bill, delayed paycheck, seasonal income drop, or emergency expense. An ongoing hardship might be permanent income loss, divorce, disability, higher insurance, higher taxes, or a payment that no longer fits the household budget.

If the hardship is temporary, a repayment plan, short forbearance, or deferral may be relevant. If the hardship is ongoing, modification or a deeper housing decision may need to be discussed. The goal is not only to stop the immediate stress, but to avoid falling behind again.

5. Prepare documents before you apply

Servicers often ask for documents when reviewing hardship options. Prepare recent pay stubs, bank statements, tax returns, unemployment proof, medical bills, divorce documents, insurance or tax increase notices, a monthly budget, and a short hardship explanation. If you are self-employed, you may need profit-and-loss information.

Only upload documents through your verified servicer portal or send them to official addresses. Be careful with any third party that asks you to send sensitive documents before you have verified who they are.

6. Do not ignore written notices

Letters from your servicer can include deadlines, document requests, rights, and warnings. Open every letter and save copies. If you do not understand a notice, call the servicer and ask what it means. If it looks like a legal or foreclosure notice, consider speaking with a qualified attorney or housing counselor quickly.

7. Avoid mortgage rescue scams

Be careful with companies or individuals who promise guaranteed modification, say they can stop foreclosure for a fee, ask you to pay upfront, tell you to stop talking to your servicer, or ask you to transfer title. Mortgage stress can make people vulnerable, and scammers often target homeowners who are behind.

8. Build a seven-day action plan

Write a simple plan for the next seven days. First, confirm the total past-due amount and deadline. Second, call your servicer and ask for available options. Third, gather documents. Fourth, consider a HUD-approved housing counselor. Fifth, decide whether your problem is temporary or long-term.

FAQ

What should I do first if I am behind on mortgage payments?

Confirm how far behind you are, then contact your mortgage servicer and ask about loss-mitigation or hardship options for your loan.

Will one missed mortgage payment cause foreclosure?

One missed payment does not automatically mean foreclosure, but it is a serious warning sign. Timelines vary, so contact your servicer and do not ignore notices.

Do I have to repay missed payments all at once?

Not always. Depending on your loan and approval, options may include repayment plan, deferral, forbearance exit options, or modification. Ask your servicer in writing.

Can I get a loan modification if I am behind?

Possibly. A modification depends on your hardship, documents, loan type, servicer rules, and investor guidelines. Approval is not guaranteed.

Should I pay a company to fix my mortgage problem?

Be cautious. Avoid upfront-fee rescue offers and guaranteed promises. Start with your servicer and consider a HUD-approved housing counselor.
