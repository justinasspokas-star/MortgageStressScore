Mortgage hardship letter

Answer first: A mortgage hardship letter should explain what happened, when the hardship started, how it affected your ability to pay, what has changed financially, and what kind of help you are requesting. Keep it clear, honest, specific, and supported by documents. Do not exaggerate, promise payments you cannot make, or send sensitive information to anyone unless you have verified they are your servicer or an official housing counselor.

A mortgage hardship letter explains why you are struggling, what changed financially, and what kind of help you are requesting. It should be clear, honest, and specific. Your servicer may use it as part of a hardship review for options such as forbearance, repayment plan, deferral, or loan modification.

Reminder: A hardship letter does not guarantee approval. Your servicer may require specific forms, documents, and review steps. This page is educational only and is not legal, financial, tax, or mortgage advice.

What to include

The best hardship letters are simple and organized. They do not need dramatic language. The goal is to help the servicer understand the hardship, your current financial situation, and the kind of review you are requesting.

  1. Your loan and contact details. Include your name, property address, and loan number if available.
  2. The hardship reason. Explain what happened: job loss, reduced hours, medical bills, divorce, death in family, tax/insurance increase, or another event.
  3. When the hardship started. Give dates or approximate timing.
  4. Your current income situation. Explain whether the problem is temporary or ongoing.
  5. What you are requesting. Ask to be reviewed for available hardship assistance, forbearance, repayment plan, deferral, or loan modification.
  6. Supporting documents. Mention pay stubs, unemployment proof, medical bills, bank statements, divorce documents, or other documents if relevant.

Simple hardship letter structure

Opening: “I am writing to request a review for mortgage assistance because I am experiencing financial hardship.”

Explanation: Describe what changed and how it affected your ability to pay.

Plan: Explain whether you expect the hardship to improve and what payment you may be able to manage.

Request: Ask the servicer to review you for available options and send requirements in writing.

Example hardship reasons

Common hardship reasons include loss of employment, reduced work hours, medical expenses, disability, divorce or separation, death of a household income earner, natural disaster, increased property taxes, increased homeowners insurance, major repairs, or business income decline. Your letter should focus on the real reason, not every financial difficulty in your life.

For example, “My hours were reduced in February and my income dropped by about 30%” is more useful than “I have money problems.” Specific details help the servicer understand the situation and compare possible options.

How long should the letter be?

A mortgage hardship letter is usually best at one page or less, unless your servicer specifically asks for more detail. The letter should be long enough to explain the hardship clearly, but short enough that the main facts are easy to understand. Use short paragraphs and plain language.

Tips

  • Keep the letter factual and respectful.
  • Use dates, amounts, and short explanations when possible.
  • Explain whether the hardship is temporary or permanent.
  • Do not blame the servicer or use emotional threats.
  • Do not promise a payment amount unless you are confident you can afford it.
  • Keep a copy of everything you send.
  • Ask for confirmation that your documents were received.

Documents that may support your letter

Your servicer may request documents to verify the hardship. These may include recent pay stubs, unemployment proof, bank statements, tax returns, medical bills, insurance notices, property tax statements, divorce documents, death certificate, profit-and-loss statement for self-employment, or a monthly budget. Only send documents through official servicer channels.

What to avoid

  • Do not send the letter to a random company that contacted you online.
  • Do not pay upfront fees to someone promising guaranteed mortgage relief.
  • Do not include unnecessary personal details that do not support the hardship review.
  • Do not leave out deadlines or notices you received from the servicer.
  • Do not submit incomplete documents if your servicer gave you a checklist.

Before you send it

Call your servicer and ask where the hardship letter should be uploaded or mailed. Ask whether there are official forms you must use. Ask for the deadline, the full document checklist, and the best way to confirm receipt. If you are confused, a HUD-approved housing counselor may help you understand the process and prepare for the conversation.

FAQ

What is a mortgage hardship letter?

It is a written explanation of the financial hardship affecting your mortgage payment and the type of assistance you are requesting from your servicer.

Does a hardship letter guarantee approval?

No. It may be part of the review, but approval depends on your servicer, loan type, program rules, documents, and financial situation.

Should I include proof with my hardship letter?

Usually yes, if your servicer requests it. Supporting documents can help verify the hardship and your current financial situation.

Can I write the hardship letter myself?

Yes. Many homeowners write it themselves. It should be honest, clear, specific, and aligned with the documents you provide.

Where do I send my hardship letter?

Send it only through your verified servicer’s official upload portal, mailing address, or instructions. Do not send sensitive documents to unverified companies.
