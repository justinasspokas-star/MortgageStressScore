Mortgage help

Need help with your mortgage payment?

Answer first: If you need help with your mortgage payment, start by checking your full monthly housing cost, contacting your mortgage servicer early, and comparing free options such as HUD-approved housing counseling, repayment plans, forbearance, deferral, and loan modification. If you are not late yet, act before the problem becomes urgent. If you are already behind, do not ignore servicer letters or deadlines.

A mortgage payment can become difficult for many reasons: job loss, reduced hours, medical bills, divorce, higher insurance, property tax increases, credit card debt, or a loan payment that no longer fits your income. The right next step depends on whether the problem is temporary, long-term, or already urgent.

Important: This page is educational only. It is not legal, financial, lending, credit, tax, or foreclosure-prevention advice. If you are behind or close to missing a payment, contact your mortgage servicer and consider speaking with a HUD-approved housing counselor.

First steps to take this week

When the payment feels too heavy, the worst move is to wait and hope the problem disappears. Start with a written review. List your mortgage payment, property taxes, homeowners insurance, HOA dues, utilities, other debts, income, and cash reserves. This gives you a clearer picture of whether the problem is a short cash-flow squeeze or a deeper affordability issue.

  1. Check your full monthly housing cost. Include principal, interest, taxes, insurance, HOA fees, utilities, and expected repairs.
  2. Check whether you are current, late, or at risk. Your options can change depending on timing.
  3. Call your mortgage servicer early. Ask what hardship options may be available before you miss more payments.
  4. Write down the reason for hardship. Examples include job loss, medical bills, divorce, insurance increase, tax increase, or reduced income.
  5. Ask for everything in writing. Keep notes, dates, names, deadlines, and document requests.
  6. Avoid upfront-fee rescue offers. Be careful with anyone promising guaranteed mortgage relief.

Understand whether the problem is temporary or long-term

This is one of the most important decisions. A temporary problem might be a short job gap, a delayed paycheck, a one-time medical bill, or a temporary family emergency. A long-term problem might be permanent income loss, unaffordable insurance, property tax increases, divorce, or a loan payment that no longer fits the household budget.

Temporary stress may be handled with budgeting, a repayment plan, short-term assistance, or forbearance. Long-term unaffordability may require deeper options such as loan modification, refinancing if you qualify, selling, renting, or changing the household budget.

Options that may be available

No single option is right for everyone. A homeowner who is current but worried may need a very different plan from someone who has already missed several payments. Your loan type, servicer rules, investor rules, income, credit, equity, and hardship reason all matter.

Repayment plan

A repayment plan may let you catch up missed payments over time by adding an extra amount to future payments. It may fit if the hardship was temporary and income has recovered.

Forbearance

Forbearance may temporarily pause or reduce payments. It can help during short-term hardship, but missed amounts usually must be handled later.

Deferral

A deferral may move past-due amounts to the end of the loan or another later date, depending on the program and loan rules.

Loan modification

A modification may change loan terms after a hardship review. It is often more relevant when the payment problem is long-term.

Refinance

A refinance replaces the loan. It may help if you qualify and the new loan improves the payment after costs, but it is not always available during hardship.

HUD-approved counseling

A housing counselor may help you understand your options and prepare for servicer conversations, often at little or no cost.

When to call your mortgage servicer

Call your servicer as soon as you believe you may not be able to pay on time. You do not have to wait until you are months behind to ask questions. Servicers may have different programs for current borrowers, recently late borrowers, and borrowers who are seriously delinquent.

Ask direct questions: “What hardship options are available for my loan?” “What documents do you need?” “What are the deadlines?” “How will this be reported?” “Will missed payments be due all at once?” “Can you send the options in writing?”

Documents to prepare

Even if you are only asking for information, it helps to prepare basic documents. You may need recent pay stubs, bank statements, tax returns, unemployment proof, medical bills, divorce documents, insurance or tax notices, hardship explanation, and a monthly budget. Do not send sensitive documents to anyone unless you are sure you are dealing with your real servicer or a verified counselor.

What to avoid

  • Ignoring letters, calls, or deadlines from your servicer.
  • Paying large upfront fees to a “mortgage rescue” company.
  • Assuming forbearance means the missed payments disappear.
  • Refinancing without checking closing costs and total interest.
  • Waiting until multiple payments are missed before asking for help.
  • Signing documents you do not understand or transferring title to someone promising help.

How to choose your next step

If you are current but worried, focus on prevention: budget review, early servicer contact, and understanding whether refinance or insurance/tax changes are driving the pressure. If you missed one payment, ask quickly about repayment, deferral, or hardship options. If you missed multiple payments or received formal notices, the situation is more urgent and you should consider speaking with a HUD-approved housing counselor.

FAQ

Can I get help before I miss a mortgage payment?

Yes. If you are worried about missing a payment, contact your servicer early and ask what options may be available before the situation becomes more urgent.

Is forbearance the same as forgiveness?

No. Forbearance usually pauses or reduces payments temporarily, but the missed amount usually has to be repaid, deferred, modified, or handled through another approved option.

Should I refinance if I’m struggling?

Only if the math works and you qualify. A refinance may lower the monthly payment, but closing costs, a new term, and total interest can change the real benefit.

Can a HUD-approved housing counselor help?

A HUD-approved housing counselor may help you understand options and prepare for servicer conversations. This can be especially useful if you are behind or confused by letters from your servicer.

What should I do if I already missed a payment?

Contact your servicer, ask about loss-mitigation options, keep records of every conversation, and do not ignore written notices or deadlines.
